# Film-world print menus: the standard (set by Jurassic Park, Oct 2026)

READ /home/claude/polish/wip/print/STYLE-LOCK.md FIRST. It is the locked house design system and overrides any rule below that conflicts with it (including the older "Hard quality gate" notes on grime, ditto, misregistration and research).

The owner is a trade-qualified chef and former restaurateur. Target: an honest 9.2 to 9.3 out of 10 against the rules below.

## The one idea (REVISED by the owner, Oct 25: this overrides anything below that conflicts)
Each piece is ALWAYS A REAL MENU: something you order food from, printed by a food venue that exists in (or fits naturally into) the film's world, in that year and place. Restaurant menu card, bill of fare, diner placemat, takeaway menu, room service card, dining car menu, bar card, cafe board card, party or banquet menu, park dining guide. NOT a prop that happens to list food (no police reports, bank slips, ledgers, memos, call sheets, newsletters, flyers, timetables, blueprints, delivery tickets, school calendars). Jurassic Park became InGen's fold-out Visitor Center dining guide with a painted island map: a real menu, beautifully designed, unmistakably from that world. Ask: where would a character in this film eat, and what menu would that place hand them?
Pick the venue from the film: a restaurant, deli, diner, bar, club, hotel, canteen or takeaway shown or named in it (renamed if it is a real business), or the obvious kind of place its characters eat. Easter eggs live in the copy, prices, names, notes and art.
PROP BITS ARE WANTED as accents on the menu (owner, Oct 25): a clipped order ticket, a rubber stamp, a tear-off coupon, a pencilled note from a character, a matchbook tucked in the corner, a ticket stub, a "PAID" stamp, a business card. Keep them small, clean and well made, and never over the dish text. Theming must be strong: someone who loves the film should feel it instantly from the venue, art, colours, names and winks. Earlier prop-object builds in these folders are a good source of Easter eggs and prop ideas to carry onto the new menu.

## Look (owner's direction)
- It must be GOOD-LOOKING and something you would be happy to print and put on the table at a movie night. Professional menu design from that era, at its best: the kind of menu people frame. Think the best real menus of the period, not the cheapest.
- Period character comes from typefaces, layout conventions, ink colours, illustration style and paper, used with taste. Wear is LIGHT: a soft paper tone and gentle edge wear at most. NO grime, toner streaks, copier faults, ditto purple, stains over text, tape, or heavy skew. A coffee ring is allowed only if faint and off the text.
- Easy to read when printed at home: dish names 20px or more in a clear display face, descriptions 16px in a clear, readable text face. No typewriter, monospace or handwriting fonts for menu body copy; a handwritten note is allowed as a small accent only.
- Art: one strong, well-made illustration or a well-treated film still where it suits (TMDB, clean crop, no people in an object that could not contain them, gentle duotone or halftone at most, never a muddy or heavy colour tint). Illustration must be skilled (engraving, line art, painted or poster style true to the period), never crude PIL shapes, stick figures or flat vector clip art. If you cannot make a piece of art look professional, use fine typography and ornament instead.
- Balanced density: full like a real menu, but with breathing room and clear hierarchy. No cramped walls of small type.

## Rules (all non-negotiable)
- Only details definitely in the film. If unsure, leave it out. In-world copy only: a piece printed inside the film cannot know the plot's later events.
- No real brand names, no real restaurants or shops, no logos other than the film's own (film logo from TMDB is fine). Fictional in-film businesses are fine only if they are not also a real business. If a real place appears in the film, use a fictional or generic name.
- No song lyrics. No em dashes or en dashes anywhere (use commas, periods, or "to"). Plain English, no AI words (elevate, indulge, iconic, nod to, homage, curated, perfect for).
- Seafood dishes stay seafood. Kids and family films are alcohol-free.
- Food must read right to a chef: specific, appetising, no repeated words between a dish name and its description, no clashing flavour treatments, period-true wording for the film's year and place, sensible prices for that world and year (a famous number from the film as one price is welcome).
- Easter eggs: minor characters, props, places, numbers, short winks (six words max). Fans should spot them; nobody else should be confused by them.
- Print readiness: real sheet size for the object (A4, letter, or a smaller real-world size like a placemat or card; say which). Body text 12pt (16px at 96dpi) where it is reading copy; nothing under 9pt; dish names clearly larger. Keep text at least 40px inside the trim. Nothing important on a fold. Produce a trim PDF plus a printer PDF with 3mm bleed, crop marks, TrimBox and BleedBox, with fonts embedded as TrueType (instance variable Google fonts to static files with fontTools as jurassic/pdfs.py and the fonts folder show). Check the PDF in two renderers (pypdfium2 and pdftoppm).
- Saved-image safe (the site uses html2canvas): no SVG for important art, no clip-path, no CSS filters on text, no vertical text, no background-clip:text, no mix-blend-mode, no inset box-shadow. Build shapes from positioned divs or painted PNG assets (Python PIL), like the Jurassic map.
- Period finish: a light paper grain and wear that suits the object (a diner placemat gets grease-proof sheen and a coffee ring, a church bulletin gets mimeograph ink, a bakery bag gets kraft paper). Subtle; text must stay crisp and readable.
- Photos (film stills, poster) stay under about 40% of the page and come from TMDB only: list them in /home/claude/polish/data/film-stills.json for the film id, download with curl from https://image.tmdb.org/t/p/original<path>. Never put people from the film into something that could not contain them.

## Hard quality gate (added after waves 1 and 2 came in under the bar)
- NO modern flat vector illustration. Simple geometric shapes, flat gradients and clean "icon" art read as a 2020s web graphic, not a printed object from the film's year. Art must look like it was printed then: line-cut or engraved drawings, halftone screens, two or three spot inks with slight misregistration, letterpress or mimeo impression, period lettering. Paint it in PIL with that texture, or use a TMDB still treated as a period halftone when the object would plausibly carry a photo.
- Film stills (owner's call): when the object would plausibly carry a photo (campaign card, newspaper, program, flyer, station handout), use a real TMDB still printed as a period halftone in the object's inks. Where the object could not hold a photo, use period art only.
- Research first: before designing, web search for 3 or more real printed pieces of the same kind from the same decade and place (e.g. "1970 railroad dining car menu", "1988 corporate party invitation"). Note what made them look real (type, inks, layout density, borders) and copy those methods, not their brands.
- Period vehicles, products and architecture must match the year. A 1970 train is not a modern high-speed train.
- Fill the object like a real one. No dead zone over about 10% of a face. Real menus are dense.
- Spelling follows the film's place: American films use "chili", "color", etc.
- Self-grades are not trusted. A separate reviewer will grade every face at full size against this gate. Anything under 9.0 is sent back. Be hard on yourself: if it looks like a template, a Canva card, or a web banner, it fails.
